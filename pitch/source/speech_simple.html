ASCLEPIUS · Pitch speech

Presented by Tula Manish · Team ASCLEPIUS · Warangal. About 3 minutes. Say the normal text out loud. The grey boxes are for you: use them only if a judge asks a technical question.

Slide 1 · Cover

So basically, my project is about helping astronauts take care of their health when their doctor is millions of kilometres away. It's an offline health app that runs on the crew's tablet on Mars. I'm Tula Manish from Warangal, and it's called ASCLEPIUS.

Imagine you're an astronaut on Mars. You wake up one morning and something feels off. Your heart's racing a bit, you didn't sleep well. Who do you ask?

Technical, if asked
  • It's a web app (PWA) built with React and TypeScript. After the first load it installs on the tablet and works fully offline: no server, no internet.
  • Two parts: the crew tablet app, and a separate Ground Station page for the flight surgeon on Earth.

Slide 2 · The problem

On Earth, you'd just see a doctor. But on Mars, your doctor is on Earth. A message takes anywhere from 4 to 22 minutes to get there, and the same again to come back. And in 2028, the Sun gets in the way and you can't talk to Earth at all for 27 days.

There's another problem too. A heart rate of 74 is totally normal for most people. But if your normal is somewhere around 52 to 62, then 74 means something's wrong. A regular health app wouldn't catch that.

Technical, if asked
  • The delays are real: we downloaded the Earth–Mars light time and the Sun angle for every day from Nov 2026 to Dec 2029 from NASA JPL Horizons. 11.0 minutes on 14 Nov 2026.
  • The 27-day blackout (8 Mar – 3 Apr 2028) is when the Sun–Earth–Mars angle is under 3°.

Slide 3 · How it works

So I built ASCLEPIUS.

Every morning, the astronaut does a quick one-minute check: blood pressure, heart rate, blood sugar, temperature, sleep and mood. The app checks each one against the normal range for adults, and against that astronaut's own normal. Whichever one looks worse, that's the answer. Green, yellow or red, on a body map. And you can tap any colour to see exactly why. No AI guessing, just clear rules.

Technical, if asked
  • A rules engine runs in a web worker (a background thread) so the screen never freezes. A full check takes about 1 ms.
  • Each reading passes 5 checks in order: is it possible → is it an emergency → normal for adults → normal for you → is it a trend. Then patterns (e.g. short sleep + high heart rate + low mood = "fatigue"). Worst colour wins.
  • "Your normal" = median ± 2.5 × MAD of your first 7 checks. We use the median, not the average, so one strange reading can't throw it off. The band's width is then fixed, so a slow decline can't hide by becoming the "new normal".
  • All thresholds live in one file, rules.json, each with an ID and a source: NASA-STD-3001 where NASA gives a number, standard medical guidelines (ACC/AHA, ADA) where it doesn't. Every card also names its NASA HRP hazard and risk.
  • Data is saved on the tablet (IndexedDB) and encrypted with AES-256. The key comes from the passphrase through PBKDF2 with 600,000 rounds.

Slide 4 · Earth Reply Clock

Now here's the part I'm most proud of. I call it the Earth Reply Clock. Every time something looks wrong, the app asks one simple question: can Earth answer before you need to act?

Say your blood sugar drops dangerously low. Earth's fastest possible reply is 27 minutes. But you need to act in 10. So the app tells you straight: don't wait for Earth. Your crew decides now, and Earth checks in later.

And the warning that goes to Earth is locked. The tablet signs it, so Earth knows it really came from the crew. It encrypts it, so only the ground station can read it. And every message is padded to the same size, so no one watching the radio link can even tell an emergency from a normal day.

Then it travels: from the tablet to the base radio station, up to a relay spacecraft orbiting Mars, across space to NASA's Deep Space Network dishes on Earth, and on to the flight surgeon's Ground Station. In my demo the radio link is simulated, but the delay is real, from NASA's JPL data.

Technical, if asked
  • Earth's answer time = light time to Earth + doctor's reply time + light time back. Example: 11 + 5 + 11 = 27 min. If the surgeon is off shift and has to be paged: 11 + 30 + 11 = 52 min.
  • Whether the surgeon is on shift depends on the time in Houston. A Mars day is 39.6 minutes longer than an Earth day (NASA's Mars24 formula), so the crew's morning keeps drifting against Houston's clock.
  • Each warning has an "act by" time: 10 min for red, 1–4 h for patterns, up to a day for yellow. Answer well before → "Earth guides". Just in time → "act, Earth confirms". Too late, or Sun blackout → "crew decides".
  • The Ground Station page holds each message until the real light time has passed (×60 speed-up for the demo) and shows the doctor exactly what the astronaut saw.
How the message to Earth is protected (if asked)

In one breath:

  • ECDH P-256: the two sides agree on a secret key without ever sending it.
  • AES-GCM: locks the message with that secret key.
  • ECDSA P-256: the signature, which proves who sent it.
  • SHA-256: a fingerprint of the message and the rules, to show nothing was changed.

Step by step:

  • 1 · Sign (who sent it). The tablet has its own secret signing key. It signs every message with ECDSA (P-256). If even one letter is changed, the signature check fails. That's how the doctor knows it's really from the crew and wasn't altered.
  • 2 · Encrypt (who can read it). The message is locked with the ground station's public key (ECDH key agreement + AES-256-GCM). Only the ground station's private key can open it, and that key never exists on the tablet.
  • 3 · Pad (hide the size). Before encryption, every message is padded to the same 4 KB block, so a red alert and a normal daily digest look identical on the radio link. This idea comes from my earlier encryption project, Hermes.
  • 4 · Number it (stop replays). Each message carries a signed sequence number. If someone re-sends an old message, the ground station rejects it.
  • 5 · Store safely. On the tablet, every record is encrypted (AES-256). The key comes from the astronaut's passphrase through PBKDF2 with 600,000 rounds, so guessing it is very slow.
  • OpenPGP (desktop version). OpenPGP is the same standard used for secure email. The optional desktop version of ASCLEPIUS uses my earlier open-source library from Hermes (built on Sequoia OpenPGP, Ed25519 signatures + X25519 encryption) instead of the browser's built-in crypto. The web app I'm submitting uses the browser's WebCrypto, with the same steps.
  • The proof it works: automated tests change one bit, forge a signature with a fake key, and replay an old message. All three are caught.

Slide 5 · Crew Pulse

I also built something for the crew's medical officer, called Crew Pulse. If one person has a headache, that's about them. But if three out of four people get headaches on the same day, it's probably not the people. It's the air. So the app tells them to check the carbon dioxide first.

Technical, if asked
  • It looks at all four crew over the last 7 days, with four rules:
  • Same complaint, 2+ people, same day → the cabin. Compared with NASA's CO2 limit of 3 mmHg (NASA-STD-3001); headaches start around 2.8 mmHg (NASA OCHMO).
  • Fever in one person, then another 1–3 days later → possible infection (NASA HRP: spaceflight can wake up dormant viruses).
  • Whole crew sleeping under 6 h for 3 days → a schedule problem. Only one person → about them.
  • Mood scores are never shown to the medical officer. The other three crew in the demo are simulated.

Slide 6 · Proof on NASA data

And I wanted to prove the "your own normal" idea actually matters, so I tested it on real NASA data: blood tests from the four Inspiration4 astronauts. The day after they landed, all four looked normal on the standard chart. But compared to their own numbers from before the flight, all four had changed. While doing that, I even found a mistake in NASA's data file, fixed it, and reported it to NASA. To be fair, that's only four people, so it backs up the idea, it doesn't prove it.

Technical, if asked
  • Data: NASA's Open Science Data Repository, study OSD-569 (Inspiration4 blood counts). We looked at red blood cell size (MCV): all 4 crew were inside the 80–100 range but below their own lowest pre-flight value.
  • 16 of 34 blood markers vary much more between people than within one person, which is exactly when a personal range beats a population range.
  • The mistake: one row (crew C003, draw L-92) was missing a value, so everything after it was shifted one column. Our Python script detects it, moves it back, and checks the percentages add up. Anyone can re-run it with "npm run proof".
  • Limits: 4 people, a 3-day flight, no tests during the flight; MCV stayed low months later in 3 crew, so a lab change may be part of it.

Slide 7 · Two-way link and impact

And Earth can answer back. On the Ground Station, the flight surgeon writes advice, signs it, and it travels back to Mars with the same real delay. The crew's tablet checks the signature before it shows the reply.

And here's the thing: this isn't only a space problem. Near Warangal, some villages are hours away from a doctor. A delay is a delay, whether it's light from Mars or a three-hour bus ride. So the same app works there too, and the daily check is in Telugu and Hindi.

And as far as I could find, there's no public NASA tool that does this. NASA's own plans say crews must decide when Earth is too far away. ASCLEPIUS turns that idea into numbers, and its Autonomy Forecast shows, day by day, when the crew will be on its own. Other projects mention the delay; ASCLEPIUS calculates it for every single day of the mission.

ASCLEPIUS works offline, it's open source, and you can try it on your phone right now.

Medicine for when Earth is too far away. Thank you!

Technical, if asked
  • Clinic mode (Settings): the Reply Clock uses travel time to the doctor and clinic hours instead of light time and the surgeon's shift. Same maths.
  • Telugu and Hindi are draft translations, waiting for native speakers to check them; anyone can add a language in about 30 minutes (CONTRIBUTING.md).
  • Open source (MIT licence), 162 automated tests, including "property tests" that throw thousands of random inputs at the engine to prove things like "chest pain is always red".

What makes ASCLEPIUS special

Say this if a judge asks "how is this different?" or "doesn't this already exist?"

What already exists
  • In this challenge I found about 60 apps on GitHub. Many have personal baselines (about 30), offline mode, a doctor's view, or an Earth-delay timer. One is very close to a normal health dashboard like ours.
  • NASA has a plan for crews doing medicine without Earth, a crew health record system, and a risk model used before missions. NASA and Google are testing a cloud AI medical assistant. None of these is a daily self-check for the astronaut.
What only ASCLEPIUS does (as far as we could find)
  • Earth Reply Clock. Others show how long a message takes. ASCLEPIUS compares that with how soon you must act, and say who decides: Earth, Earth confirms, or the crew. NASA's own plan says decisions move to the crew but gives no levels; ASCLEPIUS gives it numbers.
  • Crew Pulse. Others look at one astronaut at a time. ASCLEPIUS looks across the crew and across days: one person, the cabin air, or a fever spreading from person to person.
  • Real proof, and a fix. Others use NASA data as a picture. I ran the app's own rule on NASA's Inspiration4 blood data (4 of 4 crew changed, all looked normal on the standard range), and found, fixed and reported a shifted row in NASA's file.
  • Honest by design. No AI makes health decisions; every colour shows its rule and source, NASA's crew health standard where it gives a number. Fully offline and encrypted.
  • Built for home too. The same clock works for health workers near Warangal, with travel time instead of light time, in Telugu.
